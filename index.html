About

Yongxu Zhang is a postdoctoral associate at the Center for Neurocomputation and Machine Intelligence, Wu Tsai Institute, Yale University, working with Prof. Shreya Saxena. He builds deep learning models for complex, multimodal time series (recurrent networks, transformers and foundation models) that recover interpretable latent states in neural activity and connect them to behavior.

He received his Ph.D. in Biomedical Engineering from Yale in 2025, an M.S. in Electrical and Computer Engineering from the University of Florida in 2021, and a B.S. in Physics from Nankai University in 2018. During his Ph.D. he was the student lead of the modeling team building foundation models for neural and behavioral data at the Wu Tsai Institute. His work has appeared at NeurIPS, COSYNE and IEEE EMBC, and in IEEE TNSRE and eLife.

I'm open to research roles in industry and academia. If you have an opportunity in mind, please get in touch.

Research

Three threads run through my work. Their colors match the regimes in the figure above and mark the related papers below.

Foundation models for neural and behavioral data

As student lead of the modeling team at the Wu Tsai Institute, I developed foundation models that learn from neural activity and behavior together.

SSLD uses a transformer encoder to learn a shared neural‑behavioral latent space, and models the nonlinear switching dynamics that unfold within it.

NeurIPS 2026

Switching recurrent neural networks

Neural dynamics change with context and behavior. Switching RNNs model this by moving between discrete states, each with its own nonlinear dynamics.

  • Trained with variational inference to model nonstationary neural dynamics and find state transitions that line up with behavior.
  • Extended to input-driven switching RNNs that uncover inputs from other brain regions (manuscript under review).

NeurIPS 2024 COSYNE 2024 COSYNE 2026

Decoding behavior from neural activity

Time-varying recurrent networks classify behavior early from sequential neural recordings, and show which brain regions and features drive the decision.

  • Predicting self-initiated movements in mice seconds before they happen, from cortex-wide widefield calcium imaging.
  • Pipelines for widefield calcium imaging and fMRI time series.
